# Wheels on Fire — Redesign Prompt

## About the Brand

**Wheels on Fire** is a mountain bike guide & shuttle company on São Miguel Island, Azores, Portugal. Founded by Jallas (Swedish) and Juliana (Portuguese), who bikepacked across 4 Azorean islands and decided to stay. They offer guided MTB and EMTB tours through volcanic terrain, jungle-like forests, coastal cliffs, and ancient paths.

The brand voice is raw, adventurous, and anti-luxury: "Forget the comfort, let's get dirty." They're not selling a polished tourist experience — they're sharing the island the way they discovered it: from the saddle.

Target audience: adventure-seeking tourists and locals, from first-timers to full dirt shredders, aged 25-45, who value authentic experiences over five-star comfort.

## The Site

Build the full website for Wheels on Fire. It should feel like a premium adventure brand — editorial, confident, with real visual weight — not a generic tour operator template. The Azores' raw volcanic landscape and the energy of mountain biking should come through in the design language.

### Design Direction
- Color: red `#e61e29` as primary accent, warm neutral background (not pure white, not pure grey)
- Typography: condensed/bold display type for headings (the brand uses Futura Condensed), monospace for body text (currently Andale Mono)
- The current site uses red text outlines on headings, red separator lines, and an editorial/zine aesthetic
- Tour cards should have strong typographic names (think magazine editorial, not bland cards)
- Vary section widths and layouts — not everything centered and symmetrical
- The design should carry the raw energy of volcanic trails and ocean-ending descents

### Visual References

**Primary benchmark: H+I Adventures (mountainbikeworldwide.com)** — considered one of the best MTB tour websites in the world. Key design patterns to reference:

- **Dark, earthy palette** — dark olive/khaki background (not pure black), warm cream text (not pure white). Feels grounded and premium, like being in the forest at dusk.
- **Massive condensed typography** — enormous bold uppercase headings that dominate sections. Type IS the design — it doesn't just label, it creates visual impact. Headlines like "MTB TOURS" span the full left column at poster scale.
- **Asymmetric two-column layouts** — large heading anchored left, descriptive text floating right. Not everything is centered. The visual weight shifts across sections.
- **Tour listing with level filters** — pill/chip filters (All, Novice+, Intermediate, Intermediate+, Advanced) that filter the tour grid. Tours displayed as large editorial cards with photography + bold overlaid names + colored accent blocks.
- **Mission statement as design moment** — "We believe that adventure shapes us..." presented as a massive typographic block on dark background. The brand's why is given the same visual weight as the tours themselves.
- **Scrolling marquee text** — horizontal text strip between sections creates rhythm and energy.
- **Featured tours as editorial cards** — each tour gets: large hero photo, bold typographic name, destination tag, tour type (MTB/E-MTB), level indicator, duration (e.g. "8 days / 7 nights"). The card layouts are varied — some overlap, some have colored background panels, creating an editorial collage feel.
- **Client reviews carousel** — quotes with client name, country, and tour name. Real testimonials presented cleanly.
- **Navigation pattern** — MENU hamburger (left), centered logo, MY ACCOUNT + CONTACT US button (right). Same pattern on mobile and desktop — identical to what the WOF mockups propose.
- **Footer** — newsletter signup, contact details, clean link structure.

**What to take from H+I for WOF:** The editorial confidence, the typographic scale, the dark/warm palette approach, the tour card pattern with filters, and the asymmetric layouts. WOF should feel like a scrappier, rawer version of this — less polished luxury travel, more volcanic dirt and ocean spray. H+I is refined adventure; WOF is raw adventure.

Vertical bold condensed type as side branding. Topographic line patterns as texture element. Series/model numbering. Technical data scattered around the composition. Shows how to make gear/equipment content feel premium and tactical.

Full-bleed immersive hero photography with destination/tour cards overlaid as browseable elements. Large heading anchored to one side. Shows the card-overlay pattern for tour discovery within a hero section.

### Consolidated Design Direction

From these references, the redesign should follow these principles:

- **Contrast-driven palette with dark AND light sections.** Alternate between dark sections (warm dark, not pure black) and light sections (warm off-white/cream, not sterile white). The interplay creates rhythm and breathing room — a full-dark site would feel suffocating. Use dark for hero, brand statements, and immersive moments. Use light for informational content, pricing tables, tour details, and forms. The brand red `#e61e29` is the singular accent across both.
- **Typography IS the design.** Massive bold condensed uppercase headings at poster/billboard scale. Type overlaps, intersects, and fights with photography. It doesn't sit politely below images — it's part of the composition.
- **Two-color discipline + ground variation.** Red accent is the constant thread. Dark sections use cream/white text. Light sections use dark text. No gradients, no additional accent colors. The variety comes from alternating dark/light grounds, not from adding more colors.
- **Editorial, not template.** Asymmetric layouts. Varied compositions section to section. Photo grids that aren't uniform. Some sections bleed full-width, others are tight and contained. The rhythm changes.
- **Metadata as design element.** Tour details (level, duration, distance) presented as clean label+value pairs in horizontal strips — technical but elegant, like race timing boards.
- **Raw action photography.** Motion blur, cropped tight, gritty. Not polished tourism stock. The photos should feel like they were grabbed mid-ride.
- **Vertical/rotated type.** Tour names and section labels set vertically as side branding elements, like magazine spines.

### What NOT to do
- No all-dark site — alternate dark and light sections for contrast and readability
- No rounded cards or soft shadows — this is angular and sharp
- No stock photo aesthetics — everything should feel caught in motion
- No symmetrical grids — break the grid deliberately
- No small, polite typography — if it's not big enough to read from across the room, it's not big enough
